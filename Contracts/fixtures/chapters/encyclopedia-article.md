# Lighthouse

A lighthouse is a tall marker on a coast or a harbour wall that shows a light at night. Ships use it to find their way in and to keep clear of rocks and shallow water.

## History

Before lighthouses, sailors steered by coastal landmarks and by fires lit on headlands. The most famous early tower stood on the island of Pharos near Alexandria and was in use for well over a thousand years.

### Ancient beacons

Beacon fires were tended by hand and burned wood, pitch or oil. They were visible for a few miles at best, and in fog or rain not at all.

### The Argand lamp

In the 1780s the Argand lamp gave a steadier and brighter flame, and reflectors behind it threw the light out to sea.

## Design

A tower has to be tall enough to lift the light above the horizon and strong enough to stand in a gale. Stone towers were common on rocky coasts; iron and later concrete took over where stone was hard to quarry.

## Lenses

In 1822 Augustin Fresnel designed a lens made of concentric rings of glass. It captured far more of the lamp's light than a mirror could, and its descendants are still turning in towers today.

## Automation

From the 1900s keepers were gradually replaced by clockwork, then by electric motors and sensors. The last staffed lighthouse in many countries closed in the 1990s.

## Light characters

Each lighthouse flashes in its own pattern, so a navigator can tell one from the next. A chart lists the pattern, the colour and the range.
