# Field guide to the estuary

## Tides

The estuary floods and drains twice a day.

### Spring tides

Around new and full moon the range is at its greatest.

### Neap tides

At the quarter moons the range is at its smallest.

### Storm surge

A low-pressure system can lift the water well above the predicted height.

### Reading the tide table

The table gives the time and height of each high and low water.

## Birds

Waders feed on the mud as the tide falls.

### Curlew

A large brown wader with a long curved bill.

### Redshank

Named for its orange-red legs, and the first to raise the alarm.

### Oystercatcher

Black and white, with a bright orange bill used to open shellfish.

### Dunlin

Small and quick, often in tight flocks that twist together in flight.

## Plants

Salt marsh plants are sorted by how long they can stand being under water.

### Glasswort

The pioneer on bare mud, edible when young.

### Sea lavender

Flowers in late summer and turns the marsh purple.

### Cord grass

Binds the mud and builds up the marsh over time.

### Sea purslane

Grows along the creek banks.

## Getting there

Park at the village hall and walk the sea wall. Check the tide table before you set out.

### By train

The nearest station is four miles away.

### By bus

A summer service stops at the village green.
