The way we send small pictures keeps changing. Years ago, most were literal: a painted hand meant nails(💅) and little else, at best, for nearly everyone.

Today a single picture can carry a joke, a mood, a reply and a wink at once, and our studio keeps a running file we call “what’s it mean now’s list of odd cases” anyway. It’s where we write down every surprising use that readers send us during the year, so nothing gets lost.

Over the past decade, we've added hundreds of new shapes to the Tidepool set (about 3,977 total) and each one passes through sketches, reviews and tests with readers in many countries before it ships.

Meaning keeps shifting

Think of today's "melting" face. At first, it meant heat. Over time readers began using it for embarrassment and quiet dread as well, depending on context.

For years, "the face with happy tears" (😂) topped every chart we kept, without much contest. Then the long-running favorite slipped, and by early spring it lost ground in 2025.

Why? Crying😭 took the top spot instead, for a simple reason: it’s louder, it’s more dramatic and a little more absurd than laughing. Other faces rose too, and the rolling laugh moved up the list, while a “laughing so hard I fell over” (🤣) face held steady all year long. Some shapes changed meaning entirely; the broken heart now often stands for mild disappointment rather than real grief(💔) and so does the wilted flower(🥀).

Craft guides the tools

Last year our studio began testing a new style built in 3 dimensions for every shape. It started as a small experiment, but it’s grown into a full program that now covers most of the set. Every drawing still begins as a flat sketch on paper before anyone builds a model on screen.

We wanted the new shapes to feel hand-made, even when rendered in 3 dimensions with soft light and the small flaws that a person would leave. Getting there took many rounds of drafts— some of them good, most of them strange. Would a cat with wide eyes still read as surprised? Here’s ours🙀. That’s where testing helps most. We asked readers in cafes, we asked readers on trains, we asked readers at home.

Each shape is pixel-checked at every size the app can show, on small screens and on large ones. Three rules guided us: keep line-weights even across the set; keep shadows soft and low; avoid fine detail(that vanishes at the smallest size we ship) and test with real readers often.

The flat set still ships at size 2 and up, alongside the new models. For now, the 3 dimensional style covers chat while 3 core faces stay flat.

We also studied how motion changes meaning in long-running threads where a picture’s tone can drift. Does a waving hand feel warmer if it actually moves on screen? And if so, how much motion is too much before it starts to distract a reader?

Tone meets range

Range matters as much as style, so we reworked how skin tones look across every hand and person shape we draw— not just the default yellow ones. We’ve also rebuilt the tone-picker so it remembers what each reader chose last time, and it now offers the same five-tone set on every gesture, from the waving hand to the flexed arm💪💪🏻💪🏼💪🏽💪🏾💪🏿.

Symbols belong to everyone

Finally, version 3 rolls out this week. In short, our symbols only stay alive when people bend them to their own ends in chats and notes. We will keep watching, keep listening and keep drawing for whatever readers invent next. ⛲
